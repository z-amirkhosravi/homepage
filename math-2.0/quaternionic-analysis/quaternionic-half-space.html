    Expository

    A cusp-adapted matrix model for Sp(1,r), its Cayley transform, and the quaternionic Heisenberg boundary.

    Quaternionic Half-Space and Siegel Domains

    Discussion

    The half-space model puts a chosen cusp at infinity. Its stabilizer acts by translations, rotations and dilations, so growth, constant terms and Mellin integration can be written directly in cusp coordinates. In quaternionic dimension one the domain is {z∈ℍ:Re⁡z>0}. In every higher quaternionic dimension there is a corresponding Siegel domain, isomorphic to the ball for the same group Sp(1,r).

    The dimension is denoted by r here; spinor weight will be denoted by k. This keeps the two parameters separate. Matrices act on columns on the left, and projective coordinates are normalized by multiplication by the inverse of the last coordinate on the right.

    A Matrix Realization Adapted to Infinity#

    For an integer r≥1, put

    Jr=(00−10Ir−10−100),Gr={g∈GLr+1⁡(ℍ):g∗Jrg=Jr},

    where g∗ is quaternionic conjugate transpose. At r=1 the middle row and column are absent. For a quaternionic column ζ, put ‖ζ‖2=ζ∗ζ=∑j|ζj|2.

    For r≥1, use the quaternionic Siegel model and height function

    ℋr={(w,ζ)∈ℍ×ℍr−1:2Re⁡w>‖ζ‖2},Y(w,ζ)=Re⁡w−12‖ζ‖2.

    The projective model uses the right quaternionic line through

    v(w,ζ)=(wζ1),v∗Jrv=−2Y(w,ζ).

    For g∈Gr, write gv=(η0,η′,j)t and define

    g⋅(w,ζ)=(η0j−1,η′j−1),j(g,(w,ζ))=j.

    The identity v∗Jrv=−2Y follows by multiplication, so these are precisely the negative lines with last coordinate nonzero. An isotropic line with last coordinate zero has ζ∗ζ=0, hence ζ=0. Thus the boundary consists of Y=0 and the point ∞=[1:0:⋯:0]. The action is well-defined by QH1.

    Proposition QH1. The Action and Height Factor [2, §§3, 6]

    The projective action above is defined throughout ℋr. Its denominator and height satisfy

    j(g1g2,z)=j(g1,g2z)j(g2,z),Y(gz)=Y(z)|j(g,z)|2.(Q1)

    QH1 · version 1

    Uses Siegel coordinates, matrix model and action.

    Proof of Proposition QH1[2, (8), (18), §6]

    A vector (η0,η′,0) has Hermitian value ‖η′‖2≥0. Since gv has negative value, its last coordinate cannot vanish. Multiplying gv on the right by j−1 gives its normalized representative. A quaternionic Hermitian value scales by |j|−2 under this multiplication, proving the height formula. Apply this normalization first to g2v and then to g1(g2v) to obtain the denominator identity, with the displayed multiplication order. It also proves the action law.

    The Ball and the Same Real Group#

    Proposition QH2. Cayley Coordinates in Every Dimension [2, §§3, 6]

    Let Br={(q′,qr)∈ℍr−1×ℍ:‖q′‖2+|qr|2<1} and JB=diag⁡(Ir,−1). Define the real matrix Cr by

    Cr(q′qr1)=((qr+1)/2q′(1−qr)/2).

    Then Cr∗JrCr=JB, and conjugation by Cr identifies the ball group preserving JB with Gr. The induced bijection is

    w=(1+qr)(1−qr)−1,ζ=2q′(1−qr)−1.(Q2)

    Its inverse and height are

    qr=(w−1)(w+1)−1,q′=2ζ(w+1)−1,Y=1−‖q′‖2−|qr|2|1−qr|2.(Q3)

    QH2 · version 1

    Uses Siegel coordinates, matrix model and action.

    Proof of Proposition QH2[2, (8), (18), §6]

    The real two-by-two change in the last ball coordinate and the homogenizing coordinate takes their diagonal form to the off-diagonal block of Jr; the other coordinates are unchanged. This proves the matrix identity. Normalize the displayed vector by its last coordinate to obtain Q2. Quaternionic norm multiplicativity and

    Re⁡((1+qr)(1−qr)−1)=1−|qr|2|1−qr|2

    give Q3. The proposed inverse follows by solving Q2, and the height identity shows that it maps precisely ℋr onto Br. Conjugating a matrix preserving JB now gives a matrix preserving Jr.

    Discussion

    The signatures (r,1) and (1,r) describe isomorphic groups: multiplying the defining form by −1 does not change its isometry group. Thus Gr is a realization of the family written Sp(1,r) in this wiki. The Cayley transform changes the matrix basis and domain, not the real form of the group.

    The distinction matters arithmetically. A rational group, its lattice, and its coefficient representations must be transported together under the change of basis. A real coordinate change alone does not make every new matrix entry rational, nor does it preserve an entrywise integral-level condition verbatim. Congruence and Hecke correspondences refer to the transported rational structure.

    Translations, Dilations and Cusp Cross-Sections#

    Proposition QH3. The Quaternionic Heisenberg Translations [2, §§3, 6]

    For v∈ℍr−1 and t∈Im⁡ℍ, put

    n(v,t)=(1v∗‖v‖2/2+t0Ir−1v001),aλ=diag⁡(λ,Ir−1,λ−1)(λ>0).

    These matrices belong to Gr and fix infinity. Their actions and multiplication law are

    n(v,t)(w,ζ)=(w+v∗ζ+‖v‖2/2+t, ζ+v),(Q4)
    n(v,t)n(v′,t′)=n(v+v′,t+t′+Im⁡(v∗v′)),(Q5)
    aλ(w,ζ)=(λ2w,λζ),Y(n(v,t)z)=Y(z),Y(aλz)=λ2Y(z).(Q6)

    For r=1, N1={n(0,t)} is the additive group Im⁡ℍ. For r>1, the group Nr={n(v,t)} is two-step nilpotent, with center {n(0,t)} and commutator

    [n(v,t),n(v′,t′)]=n(0,2Im⁡(v∗v′)).(Q7)

    QH3 · version 1

    Uses Siegel coordinates, matrix model and action.

    Proof of Proposition QH3[2, (8), (18), §6]

    Multiplication gives n(v,t)∗Jrn(v,t)=Jr, using t+t‾=0; the diagonal identity for aλ follows immediately. The last denominator of n(v,t) is one, yielding Q4. In the top-right entry of a product, separate v∗v′ into real and imaginary parts. Its real part combines with ‖v‖2/2+‖v′‖2/2 to give ‖v+v′‖2/2, proving Q5. Inverses are n(−v,−t), so Q7 follows. At r>1, if v≠0, one can choose v′ with Im⁡(v∗v′)≠0, which identifies the center. Q6 follows either directly or from Q1.

    Discussion

    Every point has unique coordinates

    ζ=v,w=‖v‖2/2+t+y,y>0,

    so ℋr=Nr×ℝ>0 as a manifold. A cusp region is described by large y, with the Nr-coordinate taken modulo a lattice and, possibly, a finite holonomy group. The geometry and normalization agree with the Siegel and horospherical models discussed in [2, §§3, 6], after accounting for their factor of two in the central and height coordinates.

    At r=1, a cusp cross-section is covered by a three-torus. At r>1, it is covered by a compact quotient of the quaternionic Heisenberg group. Functions on that quotient have noncommutative Fourier analysis: characters describe the part trivial on the center, while nonzero central frequencies require additional representations. The rank-three exponential series used for the one-variable Mellin construction belongs specifically to r=1.

    The One-Variable Model#

    At r=1 write

    ℋ={z=y+x1i+x2j+x3k:y>0},G={g=(abcd):g∗(0110)g=(0110)}.

    Its action, denominator and metric are

    gz=(az+b)(cz+d)−1,J(g,z)=cz+d,ds2=dy2+dx12+dx22+dx324y2.

    Set

    nx=(1x01),ay=(y001/y),W=(0110),C=12(11−11).

    For x∈Im⁡ℍ, direct substitution gives nxay⋅1=y+x, Wz=z−1, and QH2 carries the stabilizer of the ball origin to the maximal compact subgroup

    K=StabG⁡(1)=C{diag⁡(u,v):|u|=|v|=1}C−1.

    For k∈K, put σ(k)=J(k,1). If k=Cdiag⁡(u,v)C−1, multiplication gives c=(v−u)/2 and d=(u+v)/2, hence σ(k)=c+d=v.

    Discussion

    This is a real four-dimensional half-space. The word “half-plane” is an analogy with the complex upper half-plane; it does not reduce the quaternionic variable to two real coordinates. Positive real part is convenient because the three imaginary coordinates become the translation variables. The displayed metric has curvature −4 and is the image of |dq|2/(1−|q|2)2 on the ball.

    Which Parts of the Classical Formulation Generalize?#

    Discussion

    The bounded and unbounded models for the three relevant families are:

    Family Bounded model Unbounded model
    SU(1,r) Complex r-ball Complex rank-one Siegel domain
    Sp(1,r) Quaternionic r-ball Quaternionic Siegel domain ℋr
    Sp2r(ℝ) Bounded Siegel disk Complex symmetric matrices with positive-definite imaginary part

    In complex dimension one, SU(1,1) is isomorphic to SL2(ℝ); U(1,1) has an additional central circle. The higher-dimensional complex ball and the matrix Siegel upper half-space are different symmetric spaces. Both have unbounded realizations, so passing to half-space coordinates does not select the real symplectic family.

    For quaternionic automorphic theory, a uniform formulation begins with Gr, its maximal compact subgroup Kr≃Sp(r)×Sp(1), a homogeneous coefficient bundle, and the cusp constant term. The model of the symmetric space supplies coordinates for these objects. The one-variable article below uses the explicit flat Fueter equation after its scalar frame change; in higher dimensions the invariant Schmid operator retains the connection terms.

    Let Γ⊂Gr be a congruence lattice in a rational-rank-one rational form. Set o=(1,0)∈ℋr, Kr=StabGr⁡(o), and let 𝔤r=𝔨r⊕︎𝔭r be the Cartan decomposition of this symmetric pair. Let S=ℂ2 be the standard representation of the distinguished Sp(1) factor of Kr, let U=ℂ2r be the standard representation of Sp(r), and let Vk=Symk⁡S with the other factor acting trivially. Smooth Γ-invariant sections of Gr×KrVk are represented by smooth functions F:Gr→Vk satisfying

    F(gk0)=τk(k0)−1F(g),F(γg)=F(g).

    Use the canonical homogeneous connection induced by the 𝔨r component of g−1dg. The complex cotangent representation at o is To∗ℋr⊗ℂ≃S⊗U. The projection below uses the Clebsch–Gordan decomposition of Symk⁡S⊗S.

    Vk⊗S⊗U≃(Symk+1⁡S⊕︎Symk−1⁡S)⊗U(k≥1).

    Definition QH-D3. The Schmid Operator

    For k≥1, the Schmid operator is

    𝒟k=prSymk−1⁡S⊗U⁡∘∇.

    Automorphic Conditions#

    The cusp condition is

    ∫(Γ∩Nξ)\NξF(ug)du=0for every rational cusp ξ and every g∈Gr,

    where the quotient Haar measure has total mass one. Moderate growth and central finiteness are defined as in CP-D0, using the Euclidean norm on ℍr+1.

    Discussion

    The connection in QH-D3 is part of the operator. It is carried along by a change of group realization, so no coordinate change requires treating a moving section as flat. The degree and weight dictionary is stated in QH4 below. The explicit degree-two construction and its Mellin periods are developed for r=1 in Cusp forms, Mellin transforms and parabolic cohomology.

    Proposition QH4. Weight and Cohomological Degree [1, §§1.5, 2.4–2.5]

    Let r≥1 and k≥2r, and put m=k−2r. Let πk be the quaternionic discrete series of Gr whose minimal compact type is Symk⁡S, trivial on Sp(r). Put Em=Symm⁡ℂ2r+2, the symmetric power of the standard complex representation. Then

    dimℂ⁡Hj(𝔤r,Kr;(πk)Kr⊗Em)={1j=2r,0j≠2r.

    Here (πk)Kr is the module of compact-finite smooth vectors, and relative Lie algebra cohomology is defined by the complex in CP-D3.

    QH4 · version 1

    Uses [1, §§1.5, 2.4–2.5]; CP4 for the explicit dimension-one calculation.

    Proof of Proposition QH4[1, §§1.5, 2.4–2.5]

    Order the compact Cartan coordinates with the distinguished Sp(1) coordinate first. The coefficient highest weight is λ=(m,0,…,0), and the half-sums of positive roots are

    ρ=(r+1,r,r−1,…,1),ρc=(1,r,r−1,…,1).

    The parameter λ+ρ is regular for m≥0. Its minimal compact weight is λ+2(ρ−ρc)=(k,0,…,0), identifying the specified member of the discrete-series packet. Grobner §1.5 gives one-dimensional discrete-series cohomology in the middle degree when the infinitesimal characters match; §2.4 gives that degree as 2r, and §2.5 matches the quaternionic minimal type and recalls its existence for k≥2r. The stronger k>4r assumption used for integrable matrix coefficients in his later multiplicity calculation is not needed for this relative-cohomology assertion. For r=1, CP4 supplies the compact-type calculation directly.
